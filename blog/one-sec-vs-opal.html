one sec vs Opal: The Pause or the Wall?

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

The short answer: Opal is a wall (hard blocking sessions, the deepest analytics in the category, ~$19.99/month) and one sec is a pause (a breathing exercise before flagged apps open, research-backed, ~$4.99/month). Wall people find one sec toothless; pause people find Opal oppressive. Know which you are before paying either, and know that both share one structural ceiling: it's still just you in the room.

We make PhonePact, a third thing entirely, so weigh the bias. The comparison below is played straight; our product appears only at the end, where it belongs.

What's the actual difference between one sec and Opal?

They're answering different questions.

Opal answers "how do I make the distracting apps unavailable?" It uses Apple's Screen Time framework to run scheduled focus sessions, and its Deep Focus mode is genuinely hard to break mid-session, the strongest mainstream lock on iPhone. Around it sits the best usage analytics in the category: which apps ate your Tuesday, trends across weeks, the works. Price matches the ambition: roughly $19.99 a month or $99.99 a year.

one sec answers "how do I make opening them a conscious act?" When you tap a flagged app, it inserts a short breathing exercise, then asks whether you still want in. The door stays unlocked; a doorbell gets installed. Notably, it's one of the only apps here with peer-reviewed evidence behind its mechanism, and it costs about a quarter of Opal.

Opal changes what your phone permits; one sec changes what your thumb notices.

Which one should I choose?

Pick by your failure mode, not by feature lists.

Choose Opal if your problem is long captured sessions during protected hours, work, study, sleep, and being physically kept out works for you. Data-motivated people also get real value from the analytics: seeing the number move is its own reinforcement. Be honest about price tolerance and about your history, though: if you have a track record of dismantling your own systems, you'll dismantle this one too, just a week slower.

Choose one sec if your problem is reflexive opening, the fifty autopilot taps a day, and hard walls make you feel grounded and rebellious. The pause interrupts autopilot beautifully for the first stretch. The consistently reported decay: after a few hundred repetitions, people breathe through the pause on autopilot too. one sec builds awareness but attaches no stakes to it, so awareness is where it ends.

Choose neither yet if you haven't tried the free options. ScreenZen does configurable delays at zero cost, and Apple's built-in Screen Time shows the data. Paying for a stronger version of a mechanism you haven't tested is solving a motivation problem with a purchase. The ceiling they share

Both apps, and every app in their categories, inherit one structural limit: the person who configured them can un-configure them, alone, in private, at the exact moment resolve is lowest. Opal's wall is strong until the 1am you cancels the session or lets it lapse at renewal. one sec's pause is meaningful until it's furniture. In both cases the underlying moment, you, alone, versus an engineered feed, is unchanged, because nobody else ever entered the room.

Questions people ask

Is Opal worth $99 a year?

If hard session blocking demonstrably works for you and the analytics motivate you, it's the best-in-class version of both and the price buys real polish. If you've historically disabled your own blockers, the subscription funds an arms race you'll eventually win against yourself.

Does one sec actually work?

Its mechanism has peer-reviewed support, and users consistently report it kills a large share of reflexive opens early on. The equally consistent report: the pause loses force with repetition because nothing is at stake. Best understood as an awareness tool, not an enforcement one.

Can I use one sec and Opal together?

Yes, and some people do: Opal for scheduled deep-work walls, one sec for ambient friction outside them. It's also two subscriptions for two flavors of the same solo model; consider adding accountability before adding a second lock.

What's better than both for someone who bypasses everything?

Changing who's in the loop. If you reliably defeat your own tools, the missing ingredient is a witness, not a stronger mechanism, which is the accountability-circle model and the premise PhonePact is built on.
