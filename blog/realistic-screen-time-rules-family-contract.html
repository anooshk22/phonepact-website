What Are Realistic Screen Time Rules That Teenagers Actually Agree To?

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

The short answer: teens keep rules they helped write, that apply to the whole household, that protect specific times and places rather than policing totals, and that can be renegotiated out loud. Rules imposed one-way, aimed only at the teen, enforced by surveillance, get tunneled under within a month, every time.

Most family screen rules fail in the same week for the same reason: they were written by one party, about the other party, in a moment of frustration. Below are the rules that survive contact with actual teenagers, and a contract template you can adapt tonight.

Which screen time rules actually work?

Rule 1: Phones sleep in the kitchen. Everyone's. This is the single highest-value rule in family screen management, and the best-supported: overnight phone access reliably costs sleep, and sleep loss makes everything else about adolescence worse. The rule survives because it's a place rule, not a person rule. When your phone is on the same charger, it's a household custom. When only theirs is, it's a punishment, and punishments get appealed forever. Buy two cheap alarm clocks. It's the best twenty dollars in this article, and the full routine is in our bedtime guide.

Rule 2: Protected zones, not policed totals. Arguing about daily totals invites accounting disputes, secret overtime, and an arms race you will lose. Protecting specific territories works better: no phones at the dinner table, no phones during homework blocks, no phones in bedrooms overnight. Zones are visible, so they enforce themselves socially. Totals are invisible, so they enforce themselves through spying, and spying breaks the whole system.

Rule 3: Each person sets their own daily point, and everyone's counts. Inside the protected zones, the total is the teen's own goal, chosen by them with input from you, and you set one too. This is the hinge of the entire agreement. Psychologists have documented for decades that people resist rules imposed on them (the technical term is reactance) and defend commitments they authored. A teen who picked 2h 30m will hold that line, imperfectly but genuinely, in a way they will never hold your 2h.

Rule 4: The only thing shared is the headline. When someone reaches their daily point, the family knows. That single fact, and nothing else. No app lists, no message reading, no browser history. This rule is what makes Rule 3 safe for the teen to sign: they keep the privacy adolescence genuinely requires, you keep the connection safety genuinely requires. A sentence worth writing into the contract itself: we agree to know about each other without spying on each other.

Rule 5: Anyone can call a renegotiation. Exams, friend drama, a family trip, a new job. Life changes what's realistic, and a contract with no amendment process gets abandoned instead of amended. The rule: anyone can propose a change out loud, the change gets discussed within a day, and honest raises are treated as the system working. If honest raises get lectures, you will get silent blowouts instead. That trade is always available and always worse.

The family screen contract template

Copy, argue about, and adapt. The arguing is the point; a contract nobody debated is a memo.

Our Family Screen Agreement

We're doing this together because the apps are built by professionals to keep all of us scrolling, and none of us wants to fight them alone.

  1. Phones charge in the kitchen overnight, starting at ____ pm. Everyone's, including parents'.
  2. Phone-free zones for everyone: dinner table, homework blocks (teen), and ____ (parents add one of their own, e.g. the first 30 minutes home from work).
  3. Each of us sets our own daily point: ____ (teen), ____ (parent), ____ (parent). We chose these ourselves.
  4. When someone reaches their point, the family knows. That is the only thing shared. No checking each other's apps, messages, or history. Ever.
  5. Anyone can propose changing their point or any rule, out loud, anytime. Proposals get discussed within 24 hours. Honest raises are not failures.
  6. When someone misses, the response is curiosity, not a lecture. First question: "rough day?"
  7. We review this agreement together on the first Sunday of each month.

Signed: ______ ______ ______

What makes a screen contract fail?

Four killers, in order of lethality. Asymmetry: any rule that binds the teen and exempts the parents converts the contract into a sentence, and sentences get appealed. Surveillance: the first time a parent checks an app history, Rule 4 is dead and everything built on it follows within weeks. Perfectionism: treating the first missed day as proof the system failed, when the miss-and-review loop is the system. And silence: contracts die quietly when the monthly review stops happening, so put the review in the calendar before you sign.

Questions people ask

How much screen time should a 15 year old have?

There's no evidence-backed magic number for teens; typical recreational use runs several hours daily. Better than any number: protect sleep, movement, in-person friends, and schoolwork with zone rules, and let the teen set their own total inside those protections. Owned goals get kept; assigned ones get gamed.

Should parents follow the same screen rules as kids?

For the shared rules (bedroom, dinner table), yes, and it's the strongest predictor of whether the agreement survives. Teens tolerate different totals; they do not tolerate exemptions from the household customs, and research on modeling says they're right not to.

What should be in a family screen time contract?

Overnight charging location, phone-free zones that bind everyone, each person's self-chosen daily point, exactly what gets shared (the one-fact check-in) and what never does, an amendment process anyone can invoke, and a scheduled monthly review.

What happens when my teen breaks the agreement?

Curiosity first, consequences rarely. A miss is data: wrong goal, hard week, or drifting habit. The monthly review is where patterns get addressed. Punishing individual misses teaches concealment, and concealment is the one thing the contract can't survive.
