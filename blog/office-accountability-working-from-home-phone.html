855 Remote Workers Asked How to Stay Off Their Phones at Home. The Answer Used to Sit Next to Them.

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

You were productive at the office because people were around, not because you were disciplined. Working from home removed the witness, not your character. The fix isn't a blocker or a better routine; it's putting a person back in the room: public workdays, body doubling, a standing check-in, or one friend who sees your daily screen-time number.

On r/workfromhome, somebody finally asked it straight: "I can't get off my phone when working from home - how do you keep yourself accountable?"

The thread collected 855 comments. And the honest ones kept arriving at the same answer, stated plainest here:

"While I was in the office I sat next to my boss so that kept me very accountable. Now a whole day can go by and I feel like I got nothing done."

That's it. That's the answer. The thing that kept you off your phone through years of office work was never a technique, an app, or your character. It was a person with a desk near yours. When you went remote, they didn't come with you.

And underneath those 855 comments sits the quieter confession almost nobody types out: maybe I was never disciplined at all.

The discovery nobody warns you about

Here's what going remote actually reveals. At the office, you didn't scroll for ninety minutes at your desk. Not because you had willpower, but because someone might walk past. Your boss sat within eyeshot. A coworker could glance over. You never made a decision to be focused. The room made it for you.

discipline was just company, the whole time.

Then the room emptied, and your screen time doubled, and you concluded that something was wrong with you.

Nothing is wrong with you. You lost a structural feature of your environment and replaced it with nothing. Psychologists have known for over a century that people perform differently when others are present. It's one of the oldest findings in social psychology, and it's the reason you can write in a café but not at your kitchen table. The presence of other people keeps your social self switched on. It keeps you conscious of what you're doing while you're doing it.

At home, there is no one to be conscious for. So the hand reaches for the phone before the decision does. As one remote worker put it:

"I sit down to code or study, and within 5 minutes, my hand automatically reaches for the phone."

And the worst part, the part that makes it feel less like a habit and more like a malfunction, is that it isn't even fun:

"The scary part is that I don't even enjoy scrolling anymore. I just keep doing it automatically."

Why the standard fixes keep failing

You've probably already run the standard playbook. Screen time limits: you tap "ignore." Deleting the apps: you reinstall them, or the scrolling migrates to whatever's left. A blocker: you uninstall it the first afternoon a real urge shows up. (We wrote about why that keeps happening. It's not a willpower problem, it's a locksmith problem: see the button that defeats every app blocker.)

All of those tools share one design assumption: that the fix for losing other people is more software. You had a human environment that kept you present, you lost it, and you tried to patch a social hole with a technical tool. The tool was never going to hold, because the thing that worked in the office was never technical.

One remote worker put it with unusual precision:

"a promise is you vs you at your weakest moment. friction is just physics."

A promise to yourself is an agreement between the strong morning version of you and the depleted 3pm version. And the 3pm version holds all the keys.

What actually replaces the office

Not surveillance. Not a manager dashboard. You don't need someone reading your screen. You need what you had before, which was much smaller than that: someone who would simply notice.

Some ways people rebuild it:

  • Work in public sometimes. A café, a library, a coworking day. It's the crudest version of the fix and it works for exactly the reason the office did.
  • Body doubling. Working on a video call with a friend, cameras on, both doing your own thing. Remote workers with ADHD figured this out years ago; it works for everyone.
  • A standing check-in. One colleague or friend you tell each morning what you intend to do, and each evening what happened. Not a boss. A witness.
  • Make your screen time visible to one person. Not your apps, not your browsing. Just the number. The knowledge that a real human will see "4 hours by 2pm" restores the thing the empty room deleted: a reason to stay conscious.

Notice what all four have in common. None of them block anything. None of them require willpower. They all just put a person back in the room.

Questions people ask

Why can't I stay off my phone when working from home?

Because the office was doing invisible work: the presence of other people kept your social self switched on and your hand off the phone. At home there is no one to be conscious for, so the reach happens on autopilot. It's a missing-environment problem, not a discipline problem.

How do I hold myself accountable working from home?

Borrow a person, not another app: work in public a few days a week, body double on a video call, run a two-line morning and evening check-in with one colleague, or let one trusted person see your daily screen-time total. All four work the same way: they put a witness back in the room.

Does body doubling work for phone distraction?

Yes. Body doubling means working alongside someone whose presence anchors you, on a call or in person. They don't police anything; being seen is enough to keep the autopilot reach in check. Remote workers with ADHD popularized it, and it works for most people.

Why don't app blockers fix work-from-home scrolling?

Because you administer them. On a hard afternoon you approve the extra 15 minutes, disable the block, or uninstall the app, then feel worse. A lock you hold the key to is a delay, not a lock.
