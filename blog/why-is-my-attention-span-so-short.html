Your Attention Span Isn't Shrinking. It's Being Farmed.

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated August 2026

There's a confession that shows up constantly in the forums, always in some version of the same words: I can't sit through movies anymore. I quit halfway. I check my phone during the good scenes. Something is wrong with my attention span.

Now here's the detail that breaks that theory, from a 19-year-old on r/digitalminimalism:

"I'm 19 and can spend hours reading in one session, or spend hours listening to music and focusing solely on the music, but can't sit through a movie or TV episode."

Hours of reading. Hours of focused listening. No attention deficit in sight, right up until the movie starts. If attention were simply shrinking, it would shrink everywhere. It doesn't. Which means the popular story is wrong, and the real story is stranger: your attention span isn't a dying organ. It's a crop. And for the past decade, the most sophisticated systems ever built have been farming it.

What farming means

Attention adapts to the reward schedule it's fed. That's close to literally how reinforcement works: whatever pattern of payoff your attention receives, it tunes itself to expect.

A feed pays out every few seconds, variably, forever. A film pays out over two hours, with long stretches of setup you have to sit through on trust. Feed your attention the first schedule for a few thousand hours and it recalibrates: anything that doesn't pay within seconds starts to register as broken, the way a slot machine player experiences a savings account. The movie didn't get worse. Your attention was retrained to a different payout curve, on purpose, by systems whose revenue depends on exactly that retraining. Attention researcher Gloria Mark's lab has tracked average attention on any single screen falling to under a minute. That's not a generation getting weaker. That's an industry getting better.

the harvest depends on the field never noticing.

The scale of the harvest shows up in lines like this one, from r/digitalminimalism:

"I can't watch new movies that came out since the last 5 years. I often quit watching halfway or didn't bother to start watching them."

The proof it comes back

If the shrinking story were true, recovery would be impossible. Instead, here's someone on r/nosurf describing the round trip:

"Absolutely. When I was at my worst with scrolling, I didn't have the attention span for movies either. And I'm not Gen Z. It's easy for me to get my attention span back, though, because I grew up without constant internet."

At my worst. Back. Two phrases that end the shrinking story for good, because what was trained can be retrained. (The same both-directions logic applies to the whole fog: see brain rot, and why it runs in reverse.)

How to farm it back

You are re-teaching your attention what payoffs to expect. That means reps on the slow schedule, with the fast schedule out of reach during:

  • Watch one thing with no second screen. Phone in another room, one episode or one film, and let the boring stretch in the middle be boring. The restlessness you feel at minute twenty is the old schedule protesting. It's also the reps working.
  • Read in sessions, not sentences. Twenty minutes with a physical book. The first week is genuinely uncomfortable. It stops being uncomfortable, and that change is the attention span growing back.
  • Starve the fast schedule at the margins. You don't need zero feeds. You need the feed to stop being the default filler for every gap (why the gaps matter more than the hours). Checkout lines, elevators, red lights: let them be empty.
  • Put a witness on the retraining. Solo attempts collapse quietly, because nobody sees the relapse. A daily screen-time number that one trusted person can see keeps the farm's biggest advantage (invisibility) off the field.

The farm's entire business model assumes your attention will stay where the payout is fastest. It has no defense against a person who deliberately practices the slow schedule, because the same adaptability it exploited is the thing doing the practicing.

Your attention was never lost. It was leased out. Take the lease back.

Questions people ask

Why is my attention span so short now?

Because attention tunes itself to the reward schedule it's fed, and feeds pay out every few seconds while books and films pay out over hours. After enough feed-hours, slow-payoff activities register as broken. It's retraining, not damage, and it reverses the same way it was built.

Why can't I focus on movies anymore but can focus on other things?

That mismatch is the giveaway that your attention isn't globally broken. Movies demand long stretches of unrewarded trust, the exact pattern feeds trained you out of. Activities with participation or self-paced reward (reading, games, music) often survive, which proves the capacity is intact.

How do I fix my attention span?

Daily reps on slow-reward activities with the phone out of reach: one film or episode with no second screen, twenty-minute reading sessions, gaps left empty instead of filled with the feed. Discomfort in the early sessions is the retraining happening. Most people feel movement in weeks.

Is a short attention span permanent?

No. People who reduce feed exposure and practice sustained attention consistently report the ability returning. The plasticity that shortened it is the same plasticity that rebuilds it.
