There's a Button on Your Phone That Defeats Every App Blocker Ever Made. You've Already Pressed It.

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

Every blocker ships with an exit: Ignore Limit, Forgot Passcode, Uninstall. You press it because the person being blocked is also the system administrator, and no software can guard something from its own admin. The only intervention with nothing to press is another person's knowledge: someone you trust who simply learns when you crossed the line you set.

The button goes by different names. On Apple's Screen Time popup it's called Ignore Limit. On the app your friend passcode-protected for you, it's called Forgot Passcode?. On your home screen it's called Uninstall, and it's two seconds away at all times.

Different names, one button: the exit. Every blocker ever made ships with one. It has to, because it's your phone and you're an adult. And if you've ever installed a blocker, you've pressed it. Usually late at night. Usually sooner than you'd admit.

One person on r/productivity described pressing the most elaborate version of it:

"I even had a friend set a secret passcode, but I used the 'Forgot Passcode' option to reset it to bypass the restrictions."

Read that again, because it's the whole category in miniature. This person did everything right. They knew they couldn't trust themselves with the key, so they gave the key to a friend. Real friction, real commitment. And the system still lost, because the lock itself shipped with a reset button, and the person who wanted in knew where it was.

If you've ever felt a specific, private shame about breaking your own rules like this, you're in the largest club on the internet. The confessions are everywhere, and they all have the same shape:

"I added a blocker for my WiFi for a while, but I'd just turn the WiFi off on my phone and use 4g. So I added a blocker on my phone and that takes 2 seconds to uninstall when you want to get round it."

Screen time limits? "I just find myself hitting ignore limit repeatedly." Hardware tokens? People buy a $59 device to physically lock their apps and then walk over and scan it. The blocker always loses, and it always loses the same way: the person being blocked is also the system administrator.

Every lock you hold the key to is theater

Here's the structural problem no blocker can solve. Any restriction you set up, you can take down. The uninstall button, the "ask for 15 more minutes" button, the forgot-passcode flow, the WiFi toggle. These aren't loopholes you're clever for finding. They're doors the tool has to include, because it's your phone and you're an adult.

Which means every blocker is really a bet: that the version of you who set it up will outrank the version of you who wants past it. And they never fight at the same time of day. You configure the blocker at 9am, clear-headed and optimistic. You meet it again at 11pm, tired, alone, and one tap from relief. As one remote worker put it:

"a promise is you vs you at your weakest moment. friction is just physics."

Physics gets waited out. Research on restriction bears this out: strict blocking can genuinely help in the short term, and studies show real gains in focus and well-being while the block holds. But adherence is the weak point, and usage tends to rebound when the intervention ends. The lock works until the day you take it off, and you always, eventually, take it off. Then you conclude the problem is you.

It was never you. A lock whose owner wants in is not a lock. It's a delay.

a lock you can pick from the inside was only ever a door.

What has no bypass

There's exactly one kind of intervention you can't uninstall at 11pm: another person's knowledge.

Think about what actually happened in that forgot-passcode story. The friend's passcode failed. But notice why this person had recruited a friend in the first place. Instinctively, they knew the missing piece was human. Their only mistake was the role they assigned: they made the friend a lock, and locks can be picked. The friend had nothing to do but stand between them and the phone, and so beating the system meant nothing more than beating the software.

Now run it differently. The friend isn't holding a key. There is no key, nothing is blocked, you can scroll all night if you choose. The friend will simply know. One plain message when you cross the line you set for yourself: three hours today.

You can't reset that with a forgot-passcode flow. You can't toggle it off with airplane mode. The moment has already happened; a person already knows. The only way to "bypass" a witness is to have a conversation with them, and that conversation is the entire point.

This is what the blocker era got backwards. It kept trying to make the wall stronger, when the thing that changes behavior isn't a wall at all. It's the difference between eating cookies alone in your car and eating them in front of someone who knows you're trying to stop. Same cookies. Same freedom. Completely different experience, because you're no longer invisible.

If you're building your own version

You don't need any particular product to test this. Pick one person you trust. Not a drill sergeant, someone kind. Pick a number you actually believe in (not a fantasy number; if you're at six hours, try four). Tell them the number. Then tell them, every evening, whether you crossed it. That's it. No blocking, no permission-asking, no passcode to forget.

Most people who try this notice something strange within days: they check the phone the same amount but scroll less, because somewhere in hour two the thought arrives unbidden: she's going to know. That thought is doing the job the blocker never could.

Questions people ask

Why do I keep bypassing my own app blocker?

Because you hold the keys. Every restriction you configure, you can unconfigure, and the tool must allow it since it's your phone. The blocker is a bet that morning-you outranks 11pm-you, and 11pm-you always has the admin password.

Is there an app blocker that can't be bypassed?

No, and there can't be: uninstall, safe mode, a second device, or a settings toggle always exists. The only mechanism with no bypass is a person who already knows. You can't uninstall the fact that someone saw your number.

Do app blockers work at all?

In the short term, often yes. Studies of strict blocking show real gains in focus and well-being while the block holds. The weakness is adherence: usage tends to rebound when the block ends, and the owner always ends it eventually.

What should I do instead of installing another blocker?

Pick one kind person, pick a number you actually believe in, tell them the number, and tell them each evening whether you crossed it. Nothing is blocked and nothing can be reset. That structure is what PhonePact automates.
