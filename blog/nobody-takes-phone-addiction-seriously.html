You Admitted Your Phone Use Scares You. They Said "Same." Here's What They Didn't Hear.

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

Phone addiction may be the only compulsion whose commonness is the main obstacle to getting help: confess it to a group and you'll get "same" and a laugh. You don't need the group. Behavior change runs on one specific person, jointly aware of a specific commitment, who checks in with care. Ask one friend, privately, with a structured ask, and the response is completely different.

There's a moment that people describe in the forums with a very specific kind of deflation. You finally work up the nerve to say it out loud, "I think my phone use is actually a problem," maybe to friends at dinner, maybe in a group chat, maybe to your family.

And everyone says: "same."

Someone shows their screen time report like it's a funny bar tab. Someone makes the joke about being "chronically online." The conversation moves on. And you sit there having just confessed the thing that's been quietly scaring you (the four-hour blackouts, the 2am nights, the days you can't account for) and it landed as a meme.

You weren't saying "same." You were saying help. Nobody heard the difference.

The universality trap

Here's the strange sociology of phone addiction: it may be the only compulsion whose sheer commonness is the main obstacle to treating it.

Think about how this works with anything else. Tell people you're drinking every night alone and the room goes quiet. The seriousness is socially automatic. But screen addiction is the first compulsion in history that nearly everyone shares in some measure. Average screen time keeps climbing across every demographic. Everyone's tired of their phone. Everyone jokes about it. So when you say "my screen time is out of control," the sentence has no edges. It disappears into a universal complaint, like saying you're stressed or tired.

The result is a perverse rule: the more people who have a problem, the harder it becomes for any one person to get it treated as real. "Same" isn't cruelty. It's camouflage. Your genuine emergency is wearing the same outfit as everyone else's mild annoyance.

"same" is what a group says. help arrives one person at a time.

But here's what "same" glosses over: the difference between three hours of group chats and this, from r/digitalminimalism:

"4 whole hours. just gone. i don't even remember what i watched or looked at. just swiping like an absolute zombie."

Or this, from someone on r/nosurf whose regret had become its own condition:

"6 important years that I'll never get back"

Those aren't "same." Those are people watching their one life get eaten in weekly installments, and being laughed along with when they try to say so. Meanwhile the isolation compounds the problem directly: if nobody treats it as real, you stop bringing it up. You carry it alone. And a compulsion carried alone, in secret, with no witness, is a compulsion under ideal operating conditions.

Why you need one person (not everyone)

Here's the liberating part: you don't actually need the dinner table to take you seriously. You don't need to win an argument with the culture. Behavior change research keeps landing on something much smaller. What works isn't broad social awareness of your goal. It's one specific person, jointly aware of a specific commitment, who checks in with care rather than control.

One. That's the whole requirement. And one is findable even in a world that says "same," because "same" at the group level often hides an individual who, asked directly and privately, will absolutely show up.

The mistake is in how we ask. A general confession to a general audience invites a general response. That's how you got "same" the first time. The fix is to make the ask specific, private, and structured. Something like:

"I'm not joking about this one. My phone use has genuinely gotten scary to me and I want to change it. I'm going to set a daily limit for myself. I don't need you to police me or fix anything. I just want one person to actually know how it's going. Can I tell you my number, and can you ask me about it once in a while?"

Notice everything that request rules out: no monitoring, no enforcement, no daily burden, no judgment role. You're not asking them to be a warden. That arrangement fails for its own reasons (we wrote about why). You're asking them to do the one thing the dinner table wouldn't: treat it as real.

Most people are startled by how differently a friend responds to that ask versus the group confession. "Same lol" was never the person's whole truth. It was the group's reflex. One-on-one, asked sincerely for something small and specific, people show up.

And if the honest answer is that there's no one you can ask yet, that's worth knowing too, gently. It means the phone problem is partly a loneliness problem wearing a screen-time costume, and the witness you eventually recruit may end up mattering for more than your screen time.

Your hours are real. The years are real. You were never being dramatic. You were just saying it to too many people at once.

Questions people ask

Is phone addiction real?

The compulsion loop is real and well documented: variable rewards, autopilot pickups, hours that vanish without memory. What's misleading is the social response; because nearly everyone overuses their phone a little, a serious case reads as a universal joke instead of an emergency.

Nobody takes my screen time problem seriously. What do I do?

Stop confessing to groups; groups reflexively say "same." Ask one person, privately and specifically: name the number you're setting, say you don't want policing, and ask them to simply know how it's going. One-on-one, people show up.

How do I ask a friend to hold me accountable?

Keep it small and structured: "My phone use has genuinely gotten scary to me. I'm setting a daily limit. I don't need you to fix anything; I just want one person to actually know how it's going. Can I tell you my number?" That request rules out monitoring and judgment, which is why it works.
