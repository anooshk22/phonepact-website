How to Stop Doomscrolling (and Why Bad News Feels Impossible to Quit)

By Hank Yang. Harvard Divinity School, MTS '26 · Founder of PhonePact · Built internet businesses reaching millionsUpdated July 2026
The short answer

The short answer: doomscrolling isn't an information habit, it's a threat-scanning loop. Your brain is wired to prioritize danger signals and to keep scanning until the danger resolves, and an algorithmic feed guarantees the danger never resolves, because there's always one more alarming thing. You stop it not by caring less about the world but by giving the scan an endpoint: bounded news windows, endings the feed refuses to provide, and something to do with the anxiety besides refreshing it.

You know the posture. Hunched over the phone, thumb moving, jaw tight, reading a fourth take on the same crisis, feeling measurably worse than when you started, and continuing. Not enjoying it. Continuing. Here's the machinery, because this one really isn't a willpower story.

Why do I keep reading news that makes me feel worse?

Three systems are colluding.

Negativity bias is doing its ancient job. Brains that treated threat information as top priority outlived brains that didn't, so yours is built to lock onto danger signals and hold. That wiring assumed threats were local, rare, and actionable. The feed supplies threats that are global, infinite, and mostly unactionable, and the old wiring can't tell the difference. Every alarming headline is a tiger your nervous system feels obligated to track.

The scan can't complete. The doomscroll runs on a feeling of almost: one more update and I'll understand the situation, feel prepared, achieve enough certainty to stop. But feeds are engineered against completion, infinite scroll exists precisely so there is no natural end, and certainty about an unfolding crisis is not for sale at any scroll depth. So the checking behavior gets the anxiety-relief promise without ever paying it out, which is, not coincidentally, the variable-reward structure that makes slot machines work.

Anxiety recruits the behavior that feeds it. Scrolling bad news raises your baseline anxiety, and anxiety's favorite move is scanning for threats, which means the doomscroll manufactures its own demand. Passive feed consumption tracks with lower wellbeing in the research generally (Kross and colleagues documented the pattern back in 2013), and threat-flavored passive consumption is the strongest version of the effect.

doomscrolling is checking the weather forever instead of ever grabbing an umbrella.

How do I actually stop doomscrolling?

Give news an appointment and a container. The goal isn't ignorance, it's bounded intake: one or two windows a day, 15 to 20 minutes, ideally not first thing and never last thing (nighttime doomscrolling has its own gravity, covered in our revenge bedtime piece). Inside the window, prefer sources with endings, a daily briefing email, a news podcast episode, a front page, over feeds. An episode finishes. A feed is designed not to.

Consider quitting the worst source outright. If one app supplies most of your threat feed, the realistic timeline of leaving it is worth reading before you decide.

Kill the drip. News app notifications, breaking-news badges, and the news-adjacent accounts in your social feeds are a threat-signal IV line. Turn off every news notification that isn't a genuine local emergency channel. If a crisis is big enough to matter, it will still be fully available at your next appointment; nothing about your Tuesday required the push alert.

Close the loop with one action. The scan continues because it can't complete, so complete it manually. After your news window, do one thing that converts concern into agency, however small: the donation, the call, the email, the decision that this one isn't yours to carry today, written down. Action is the only real off-switch for threat arousal. Information never was.

Notice the reach, name the feeling. Doomscrolling sessions mostly begin as ordinary anxious pickups. The mid-reach question, "what am I feeling right now?", catches a surprising share of them before the feed gets its hooks in, and "anxious about things I can't control" is an answer with better responses available than scrolling: the walk, the shower, the human.

Don't fight it in private. Like every invisible phone habit, doomscrolling thrives on nobody knowing. A daily point held with one person or a small circle, where they simply learn when you've reached the amount you chose, gives the endless scan the one thing the feed never will: an external endpoint, held warmly by someone who wants your evening back as much as you do. Questions people ask

Why is doomscrolling so addictive?

It combines negativity bias (threat information gets priority attention), an uncompletable goal (certainty about unfolding events), and variable rewards on infinite scroll. The behavior promises anxiety relief and structurally never delivers it, which keeps the loop spinning.

Is doomscrolling bad for your mental health?

Passive consumption of negative feeds is consistently associated with higher anxiety and lower wellbeing, and it compounds: the anxiety it produces drives more threat-scanning. Bounding intake reliably improves how people feel within days.

How do I stay informed without doomscrolling?

Use sources with endings on a schedule: a daily briefing, one podcast episode, a front page, in one or two 15-to-20-minute windows. Kill news notifications entirely. Depth on a schedule beats drip-feed always, for both knowledge and sanity.

Why do I doomscroll at night especially?

Nighttime combines an empty self-control tank, the day's suppressed worries surfacing in the quiet, and a phone within reach. The kitchen charger plus a five-minute wind-down ritual removes the setup; the bounded news window handles the rest.
