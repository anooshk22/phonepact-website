FOLIO 01 // LEAD RESEARCH

Neurodivergence & The Failure of Rigid Blockers

Hariadi, Chow & McGrenere · ACM CHI 2026 · DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790801

Interviews with 27 neurodivergent post-secondary students found tension between fixed, linear blocker designs and nonlinear work patterns such as hyperfocus.

Conventional blockers operate on rigid, all-or-nothing schedules that disregard executive constraints, time blindness, and the unique cognitive momentum of hyperfocus states (typical in ADHD and Autistic profiles). When users are abruptly locked out during flow, it shatters momentum and shames their capabilities, prompting them to abandon the tool completely.

CORE FINDINGS

I

Psychological Backlash

All-or-nothing blockers trigger executive shame and feelings of failure, reminding neurodivergent users of cognitive differences and fueling a cycle of negative self-evaluation.

II

The Stimming Failure

Blockers offer no middle ground for "digital stimming" (brief, controlled breaks to soothe overstimulated minds), driving users to uninstall or disable the apps out of frustration.

III

Hyperfocus Disruptions

Hard locks trigger abrupt task-switching. Because transitioning attention requires high executive function, a rigid schedule shatters momentum and triggers immediate abandonment.

02 / FLAWS

What Intervention Research Suggests

Specific studies that inform PhonePact’s emphasis on personalization, awareness, and user choice.

Journal of Medical Internet Research

1. Goal-Directed Smartphone Use

+

Methodology: Randomized controlled trial of a theory-based mobile intervention for goal-directed smartphone use.

Finding: The intervention was associated with lower problematic smartphone use and less time spent on the phone.

Design implication: Support concrete goals and self-efficacy rather than treating awareness as an end in itself.

Keller et al. · JMIR mHealth (2021) · DOI 10.2196/26397
Computers in Human Behavior

2. Notification Timing Matters

+

Methodology: Two-week randomized field experiment with 237 smartphone users.

Finding: Delivering notifications in three daily batches improved several reported outcomes, while turning notifications off entirely increased anxiety and fear of missing out.

Design implication: Interventions should consider timing and context instead of assuming that more restriction is always better.

Fitz et al. · Computers in Human Behavior (2019)
JMIR mHealth and uHealth

3. Personalized Nudges and Choice

+

Methodology: Three-week randomized controlled trial with 70 iPhone users using personalized reminders that allowed them to quit or continue.

Finding: Time on participants’ most problematic app fell by about 29 minutes per day, although problematic social-media use and self-efficacy did not significantly change.

Design implication: Personalized friction can help, but short-term screen-time reduction is not the same as durable self-regulation.

Mertens et al. · JMIR mHealth (2026) · DOI 10.2196/56824
Frontiers in Public Health

4. Social Comparison Is Not One-Size-Fits-All

+

Methodology: Survey and structural-equation analysis of 1,452 adult fitness-app users.

Finding: The relationship between upward comparison and well-being differed with users’ self-control, underscoring that comparison can help some people and harm others.

Design implication: Use private support and encouragement rather than public rankings or universal streaks.

Cai & Li · Frontiers in Public Health (2024)
03 / CONSTRAINTS

Constraints and Tradeoffs

Research and reviews that temper simple claims about lockouts, willpower, detoxes, and long-term engagement.

Behavioral Psychology

1. The Theory of Psychological Reactance

+

Methodology: Literature review and survey examining causes of psychological reactance in human-computer interaction.

Finding: Interfaces can provoke resistance when people perceive threats to autonomy, particularly when systems feel controlling or opaque.

Design implication: Preserve meaningful choice, explain interventions, and avoid claiming that every lockout produces the same response.

Roubroeks et al. · ACM HCI (2017)
Cognitive Energy & Self-Control

2. Self-Regulatory Fatigue (SRF)

+

Methodology: Cross-cultural path analysis of public employees in China (N=205) and the United States (N=210).

Finding: Self-reported self-control depletion mediated the association between after-hours work smartphone use and bedtime procrastination in both samples.

Georgetown University Research

3. The Moderation Paradox

+

Methodology: Evaluation of strict digital lockouts vs. moderate limits.

The Core Flaw: Demanding absolute behavioral compliance (e.g., strict detoxes).

What the evidence says: Reviews find that digital-detox interventions often reduce device use, while their effects on broader well-being remain mixed.

Design implication: Prefer flexible, measurable reductions over promising that one strict method works for everyone.

Digital Detox and Well-Being · Review (2024)
JMIR mHealth and uHealth

4. Continued Use Depends on Fit

+

Methodology: Longitudinal qualitative study of continued use decisions among mobile-health app users.

Finding: Continued use reflected both commitment to a health goal and users’ assessment of app fit, including interface, notifications, data collection, goal management, and actionable guidance.

Design implication: Retention should come from usefulness and fit—not simply from adding more engagement mechanics.

Vaghefi & Tulu · JMIR mHealth (2019)
